## 1. Executive decision

The project will pursue one channel concept combining three compatible modes:

1. **Oddly satisfying visual sequences** — immediate sensory appeal, rhythm, symmetry, motion, impact, and a clear payoff.
2. **Physics simulations** — visually convincing interactions between rigid bodies, soft bodies, particles, fluids, gravity, friction, elasticity, collisions, and chain reactions, within the limits of the chosen simulation method.
3. **Hypothesis-led visual experiments** — a clear question, controlled changes, a visible test, and a result that viewers want to see.

These are not three unrelated content niches. They are three ingredients in one editorial promise:

**“Beautiful visual experiments that test how a system behaves and reveal a satisfying, surprising, or counterintuitive result.”**

The working product category is **Visual Physics Experiments**. This is an internal descriptive label, not a finalized public channel name or trademark.

### Strategic choice

Do not build a generic channel that merely publishes colorful moving objects. Use visual satisfaction as the hook, physical interaction as the spectacle, and a question or rule as the episode’s distinctive idea.

The channel should be:
- Understandable with the sound off.
- Visually legible within the first second.
- Built around an original setup, rule, constraint, or outcome.
- Repeatable without recurring characters, dialogue-heavy storytelling, or complex character continuity.
- Suitable for Shorts first, with proven ideas expandable into longer experiments.
- Created with documented human editorial review, not unattended mass production.

### What is approved and what is not

| Decision | Status |
|---|---|
| Combine satisfying visuals, physics simulation, and hypothesis-led experiments in one channel | **APPROVED** |
| Start with Shorts | **APPROVED** |
| Target a global audience with English metadata and minimal language dependence | **APPROVED** |
| Develop long-form videos after the Shorts pilot provides evidence | **APPROVED IN PRINCIPLE** |
| Public channel name, logo, and final visual identity | NOT SELECTED |
| Specific simulation engine, renderer, AI models, voice, or editing stack | NOT SELECTED |
| Production automation or unattended publishing | NOT APPROVED |
| Claims about likely views, revenue, retention, or monetization eligibility | NOT ESTABLISHED |
| Full-scale production | BLOCKED until the prototype gate is passed |

## 2. Product definition and audience promise

### 2.1 Product definition

Each episode presents a self-contained visual system. The viewer sees an object, arrangement, or mechanism behave under clear conditions. The episode produces at least one of the following:

- A satisfying physical motion or pattern.
- A visually striking interaction.
- A result that differs from a reasonable expectation.
- A comparison that reveals the effect of changing one variable.
- A chain reaction with a clear causal progression.
- A controlled escalation toward a visible payoff.

The content should not require the viewer to know physics terminology. When a concept needs explanation, use a simple label, visual comparison, or concise English line rather than a long lecture.

### 2.2 Target audience

**Primary target:** General global viewers who enjoy visual entertainment, simulations, puzzles of cause and effect, and satisfying motion.

Age distribution and country mix are unknown until channel analytics are available. Do not assume that “understandable to children” means the channel is automatically child-directed. The creator must accurately classify each video and the channel under YouTube’s audience rules based on actual intended audience and content.

### 2.3 Viewer promise

Every video should quickly answer one of these questions:
- What is about to happen?
- Can this system complete the challenge?
- Which of these setups will perform better?
- What changes if one physical rule or parameter changes?
- Can a chain reaction reach the final target?
- What is the most satisfying outcome this system can produce?

The viewer should receive a visible payoff, not an empty tease.

### 2.4 Content boundaries

**In scope**
- Marble and particle systems.
- Domino-like chain reactions and original mechanisms.
- Rigid-body collisions and controlled destruction.
- Gravity, friction, bounce, elasticity, balance, momentum, and energy-transfer demonstrations.
- Soft-body or fluid-like behavior where simulation quality is sufficient.
- Repeating patterns, sorting, stacking, packing, cascading, and controlled collapse.
- Side-by-side experiments with one variable changed.
- Impossible-in-real-life setups clearly framed as simulations.
- Visually satisfying loops when the loop itself is intentional and well designed.

**Out of scope unless separately approved**
- Multi-character narrative films.
- Generic AI-generated clips with no controlled concept or original direction.
- Fake footage presented as a real physical event.
- Unverified scientific claims presented as fact.
- Reuploads, near-copies, or superficial recolors of competitor videos.
- Long compilations that add no new structure or value.
- A mixed channel containing unrelated topics such as general trivia, animal battles, news, or business explainers.

## 3. The unified content architecture

The three modes should share the same visual language, world, simulation assets, and editorial promise. They are not separate channels.

### Pillar A — Satisfying Systems

**Purpose:** Earn attention through immediate motion, rhythm, visual order, and payoff.

Examples:
- Hundreds of balls pass through a custom mechanism and settle into a precise pattern.
- A carefully designed sequence fills a geometric grid without gaps.
- A chain reaction creates a symmetrical final arrangement.
- A loop returns seamlessly to its initial state.

**Required differentiator:** The system, mechanism, choreography, or final arrangement must be original. Color changes alone do not constitute a new episode.

### Pillar B — Physics in Action

**Purpose:** Make the physical interaction itself the spectacle.

Examples:
- Compare bouncy, soft, heavy, and light objects in the same setup.
- Show a structure reacting to a sequence of impacts.
- Compare how different surfaces alter a rolling object’s path.
- Explore deformation, collisions, stacking, or controlled collapse.

**Required differentiator:** The interaction must be intentional and visually credible. If the simulation is approximate, do not imply it is a precise scientific prediction.

### Pillar C — The Experiment

**Purpose:** Add a question, constraint, or prediction that gives the viewer a reason to wait for the result.

Examples:
- Which of three designs transfers a ball to the target fastest?
- Can a small object trigger a larger chain reaction?
- What changes when gravity is increased but all other settings stay constant?
- Which structure survives a specified sequence of impacts?
- What is the smallest change that makes a stable arrangement collapse?

**Required differentiator:** A meaningful experimental variable and a clear result. If several factors change simultaneously, do not claim that one factor caused the result.

### Initial pilot mix — proposed, not a permanent rule

Use this starting distribution for the first comparable test batch:

| Pillar | Share of pilot | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Satisfying Systems | 40% | Test immediate visual appeal and rewatchability |
| Physics in Action | 30% | Test the spectacle of physical behavior |
| The Experiment | 30% | Test whether a clear question improves interest and audience response |

The mix may change only after reviewing a sufficiently comparable set of published results and production costs. This allocation is a test design, not evidence that one pillar is superior.

### Unifying rule

Every upload must look and feel like part of the same channel. Consistency comes from rendering style, framing, object design, pacing, sound treatment, and standard of experimentation—not from repeating the same scene with superficial variations.

## 4. What makes the product distinctive

The niche already has competitors. “Satisfying physics” alone is not a defensible advantage. The product must build a recognizable editorial and visual system.

### 4.1 Differentiation principles

1. **Original mechanisms:** Design a new arrangement, rule, constraint, or causal chain for each episode.
2. **Visible stakes:** Give the viewer a target, boundary, countdown, fragile balance, race, or other clear reason to care about the outcome.
3. **A specific payoff:** The ending should resolve the setup, complete a pattern, reveal a winner, or demonstrate a meaningful difference.
4. **Controlled comparisons:** When making a comparison, change one variable at a time wherever practical.
5. **Distinct visual identity:** Use a consistent material palette, lighting logic, camera behavior, typography, framing, and sound signature. Do not copy a competitor’s identifiable style.
6. **Credible motion:** Objects should not visibly teleport, intersect, change mass, or violate established rules without an explicit creative reason.
7. **Meaningful variation:** A new episode needs a new visual problem or outcome—not merely a different color, object skin, or camera angle.
8. **A library of reusable components:** Reuse tools and assets where helpful, but compose them into materially different scenes.
9. **Human creative direction:** AI may assist research, scripting, asset generation, and code, but a human must select the premise, review the result, and reject weak outputs.
10. **No false claims of realism:** Clearly distinguish artistic simulation from measured physical prediction.

### 4.2 The “why this video?” test

Before approving a concept, complete this sentence:

> “This episode is worth watching because it shows ______ in a way that viewers cannot get merely by watching a generic ball, domino, or collision animation.”

If the blank can only be filled with “different colors,” “more objects,” or “another satisfying loop,” reject or redesign the concept.

### 4.3 Originality scorecard

Score each item from 0 to 2. Maximum 10.

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original premise | Generic copy or recolor | Familiar premise with a modest twist | Distinct setup, rule, or question |
| Visual identity | Interchangeable | Some recognizable choices | Coherent and distinctive |
| Physical/causal progression | Random motion | Basic progression | Clear, deliberate chain of events |
| Payoff | No clear resolution | Adequate ending | Strong, earned, visually legible payoff |
| Episode-specific value | Repeats previous episode | Some variation | Adds a genuinely new experience |

**Release gate:** Minimum 8/10, with no score of 0 for original premise or episode-specific value. This is a proposed internal quality threshold; it is not a predictor of virality.

## 5. Shorts product specification

### 5.1 Duration and format

- Initial target duration: approximately **20–45 seconds**. Treat this as a production hypothesis, not a platform requirement.
- Primary aspect ratio: **9:16 vertical**.
- Keep important action inside a safe central region so interface overlays do not hide it.
- Use readable, minimal on-screen text.
- Design for sound-off comprehension; sound should improve the experience, not be necessary to understand the basic action.
- Export settings and resolution should be selected after the first render benchmark and checked against current YouTube upload guidance.

### 5.2 Recommended narrative structure

This is a flexible editorial pattern, not a rigid template to stamp onto every video.

1. **0–1.5 seconds — Immediate visual hook:** Show the unusual system, near-failure, target, or key motion immediately. Avoid a logo intro.
2. **1.5–5 seconds — Establish the rule:** Make the goal or constraint visually obvious. Use a short caption only if needed.
3. **5–25 seconds — Escalation or test:** Let the mechanism act. Build progression, variation, tension, or complexity.
4. **Final segment — Payoff:** Show the result clearly. Do not cut away before the result is understandable.
5. **Optional loop:** If a seamless loop improves the experience, align the final state and opening state deliberately. Do not force a loop where it weakens the payoff.

The timing should flex to the experiment. A simple satisfying loop may need almost no explanation; a comparative test may need more setup.

### 5.3 Shorts quality checklist

A Short must pass all mandatory items:
- The main action begins promptly.
- The viewer can infer what is happening without a long introduction.
- The setup, physical rule, and intended outcome are internally consistent.
- No obvious collision glitches, object teleportation, flicker, broken shadows, or accidental clipping.
- The main event is legible on a phone screen.
- The ending delivers a clear result.
- Audio has no clipping, harsh transient spikes, accidental silence, or unlicensed material.
- Captions and text have correct spelling and remain readable.
- The episode passes the originality scorecard.
- Music, sound effects, fonts, models, textures, and other assets have documented usage rights.
- The final export has been watched from beginning to end by a human reviewer.

### 5.4 Sound design

Prefer custom or properly licensed sound:
- Light, controlled impacts.
- Rolling, bouncing, sliding, snapping, or mechanical sounds appropriate to the action.
- A restrained low-frequency accent at a key payoff, where suitable.
- Intentional silence when it improves contrast.

Avoid a generic stock sound on every collision. Sound should support material, weight, speed, and timing. Do not claim that a sound is a real recording of the simulated event unless it is.

## 6. Long-form expansion

Long-form is a second stage, not a requirement for the first release.

### 6.1 Entry condition

Do not assume that a successful Short will automatically support a successful long video. Expand only when:
- A concept or series has repeated audience interest, not just one isolated spike.
- There is enough meaningful material for a longer narrative.
- The long version can add experiments, comparisons, explanation, or escalation rather than padding.
- The expected workload is reasonable for the project.

### 6.2 Long-form formats

**Format 1 — One question, many experiments**
- Establish one central question.
- Test several distinct configurations.
- Explain the variables and compare results.
- End with the most informative or visually satisfying result.

**Format 2 — Build and break**
- Start with a target or constraint.
- Construct a sequence or system in stages.
- Test the design.
- Diagnose failure and redesign.
- Show the final attempt.

**Format 3 — The physics tournament**
- Several mechanisms attempt the same task.
- Use consistent rules and measurable criteria.
- Show each attempt and the final comparison.
- Avoid pretending that arbitrary game-like scores are scientific measurements.

**Format 4 — From simple to extreme**
- Begin with a simple physical behavior.
- Increase one variable or difficulty dimension in stages.
- Keep the progression coherent and visually understandable.
- Clearly label when settings are exaggerated for entertainment.

### 6.3 Long-form quality requirements

- A clear question or thesis, not a loose compilation.
- A distinct progression across the episode.
- Enough new information or visual variation to justify the duration.
- No padding, repeated shots, or artificial slow motion used solely to increase runtime.
- A conclusion that follows from the demonstrations.
- Where the video makes factual claims, use accurate sources and distinguish simulated outcomes from real-world evidence.
- Reuse Short scenes only where they serve the longer narrative; do not simply stitch together previously published clips.

## 7. Production workflow

The workflow should be modular, restartable, and reviewable. Automate repetitive mechanics only after the creative process proves repeatable.

### Stage 1 — Idea intake and scoring

For each candidate episode, record:
- Working title.
- Content pillar.
- Viewer question or visual premise.
- What the viewer sees in the first second.
- What changes, what remains constant, and what counts as success.
- Intended payoff.
- Why this is not interchangeable with an existing episode or competitor example.
- Estimated scene complexity and likely failure modes.
- Required assets and licenses.
- Whether claims of real physics or scientific accuracy will be made.

Reject concepts with no distinct premise or no clear payoff.

### Stage 2 — Simulation plan

Before building the scene, define:
- Objects and their properties.
- Geometry and scale.
- Starting positions and initial conditions.
- Gravity, friction, restitution, constraints, and other relevant parameters.
- The intended sequence of events.
- Camera position and framing.
- Expected result and possible alternate outcomes.
- Which elements are physically simulated and which are art-directed.

For hypothesis-led episodes, change one variable at a time wherever possible. Record parameter values so the result can be reproduced.

### Stage 3 — Prototype scene

Build the smallest scene that can answer the visual question. Use simple placeholder geometry first. Do not spend time on final materials, detailed textures, or advanced rendering until the core action works.

Review:
- Does the mechanism work?
- Is the action visible in the intended frame?
- Does the result read immediately?
- Does the setup create enough anticipation?
- Does the scene fail in a way that can be fixed deterministically?

### Stage 4 — Simulation and visual debugging

Run a low-cost preview before the final render.

Inspect:
- Penetration or interpenetration.
- Unstable stacks and unintended jitter.
- Objects moving without an apparent cause.
- Tunneling through thin objects.
- Inconsistent collision behavior.
- Exploding or diverging simulations.
- Incorrect scale or camera clipping.
- Unintended differences between comparison runs.

If the selected engine cannot model the behavior credibly, simplify the premise or label the scene as stylized. Do not disguise technical failure as a scientific result.

### Stage 5 — Art direction and rendering

Apply the channel’s approved visual system:
- Consistent materials and palette.
- Clean silhouettes and contrast.
- Controlled lighting and shadows.
- Intentional camera movement.
- Composition designed for vertical viewing.
- Enough visual detail to feel polished, but not so much that the main action becomes hard to read.

Use expensive rendering features only when a preview shows a material improvement. Prefer predictable, reproducible renders over a complex pipeline whose quality is difficult to control.

### Stage 6 — Sound, captions, and edit

- Add sound effects that match timing and apparent material.
- Add minimal English labels where they improve comprehension.
- Remove dead time, confusing transitions, and redundant shots.
- Check the first second, the final payoff, and any loop transition separately.
- Do not use a voiceover by default. Use narration only when it adds meaningful clarity or context.

### Stage 7 — Quality and originality review

A human reviewer must verify:
- Visual coherence and technical correctness.
- The intended premise is clear.
- The result matches the setup.
- Any factual statement is supported.
- The scene is original enough to pass the scorecard.
- No copyrighted or unlicensed material has slipped in.
- The video is not merely a lightly altered version of another upload.
- The video meets platform rules and is appropriately audience-classified.

### Stage 8 — Export, archive, and publish

For each released video, retain:
- Concept record and score.
- Script or on-screen text.
- Scene source and simulation parameters.
- Source assets and license records.
- Render settings.
- Final video file.
- Thumbnail/cover frame where relevant.
- Audio asset records.
- Quality-control checklist.
- Publication URL and date.
- Available performance measurements at each review date.

The local persistent project archive remains the source of truth. Temporary GPU sessions must not be treated as permanent storage.

## 8. Proposed technical architecture

No specific tool or model is approved merely because it appears in this document. Select the stack through a small benchmark using the actual hardware and intended scene complexity.

### 8.1 Design principles

- **Deterministic core:** Prefer a controllable simulation or procedural scene for the main action.
- **GPU optional:** Use a GPU when it provides a demonstrated quality or speed advantage. Do not make text-to-image or text-to-video generation a foundational dependency without evidence.
- **Reusable components:** Separate object definitions, physical parameters, scene logic, camera, lighting, audio, and export settings.
- **Parameter-driven variations:** Change experiment variables through explicit parameters rather than hand-editing every scene.
- **Reproducibility:** Record software versions, random seeds where applicable, parameters, and render settings.
- **Human approval:** Automated generation may create candidates, but it must not automatically publish without review.
- **Recoverability:** Every stage should produce explicit outputs and be restartable without losing prior work.

### 8.2 Engine selection criteria

Benchmark candidate tools against the same small scene. Compare:
- Physical behavior and stability.
- Control over collision properties and constraints.
- Ease of scripting or procedural scene creation.
- Ability to render vertical output.
- Preview and final render time.
- CPU/GPU requirements and memory use.
- Determinism and reproducibility.
- Batch execution and error reporting.
- Export quality and post-production integration.
- License compatibility for commercial use.
- Documentation and maintainability.

Do not select an engine based on feature lists alone. Record the actual test scene, hardware, settings, time, failures, and resulting frames.

### 8.3 Suggested module boundaries

1. **Idea specification:** episode premise, pillar, success criteria, and originality score.
2. **Scene specification:** objects, geometry, materials, camera, and timeline.
3. **Physics configuration:** parameters and constraints.
4. **Simulation runner:** scene execution and error detection.
5. **Preview renderer:** low-cost review output.
6. **Final renderer:** approved quality settings.
7. **Audio assembly:** licensed/custom effects and timing.
8. **Video compositor:** vertical framing, captions, transitions, and encoding.
9. **Quality gate:** technical checks plus a human review record.
10. **Asset manifest:** asset origins, license notes, checksums, and version information.
11. **Metrics log:** publication data and observation dates.

These are proposed logical responsibilities, not a command to create code immediately. Do not build all modules until the prototype proves the need.

### 8.4 Compute and storage

- Benchmark on the actual local machine first where practical.
- Treat Kaggle or any temporary notebook runtime as an execution environment, not permanent storage or an always-on server.
- Do not assume current accelerator availability, quotas, session duration, or filesystem persistence.
- Save essential source files, parameters, logs, and outputs to durable storage after each meaningful stage.
- Keep dependency versions and installation instructions.
- Capture failure logs and a minimal reproducible scene for debugging.
- Avoid downloading or running large model weights until the model’s identity, source, license, hardware requirements, and use case are confirmed.

## 9. Originality, rights, and YouTube monetization risk

No channel concept can be guaranteed monetizable in advance. The product should be designed to offer genuine original entertainment, not to evade automated detection.

YouTube’s official monetization policy discusses “inauthentic content,” including repetitive or mass-produced content, and separately addresses reused content. Review the current official policy before publication:
https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392

### Required safeguards

- Do not reproduce another creator’s exact scene, sequence, script, sound design, or distinctive presentation.
- Do not assume that changing colors, materials, speed, or camera angle makes a copied scene original.
- Preserve evidence of original scene design, parameters, and editorial decisions.
- Track licenses for models, textures, fonts, sound effects, and third-party assets.
- Check commercial-use conditions for every external tool, model, and asset.
- Do not present synthetic simulation as real footage.
- Do not make claims about real-world physics beyond what the simulation and evidence support.
- Avoid automated bulk publishing of near-identical videos.
- Ensure each episode has a distinct premise and meaningful viewer value.
- Review the audience designation accurately. “Family-friendly” does not automatically mean “made for kids,” and child-directed content must not be misclassified to preserve features or revenue.

**Policy posture:** Optimize for substantive originality, transparent presentation, and human quality review. Do not optimize for avoiding detection.

## 10. Competitive research and evidence standard

The broad market contains successful marble runs, domino simulations, satisfying loops, and physics animations. That establishes the existence of demand, not the probability that this new channel will succeed.

Any future competitor research must use traceable examples and separate public facts from inference.

### 10.1 Required evidence ledger

For each sampled video, record:
- Exact title and video URL.
- Channel URL.
- Publication date and observation date.
- Visible views and duration.
- Visible channel subscriber count, if available.
- Content pillar and premise.
- Whether the video is a Short or long-form.
- Whether the content appears original, live-action, procedural, simulated, or compiled.
- What differentiates it.
- Comparable videos from the same channel.
- Low-performing counterexamples where discoverable.
- Source limitations and confidence.

### 10.2 Comparison rules

- Do not compare raw view totals without considering upload age, channel size, and format.
- Do not infer retention, click-through rate, traffic source, RPM, or revenue from public views.
- Treat third-party channel statistics as estimates and label them accordingly.
- Do not use one viral video as proof of repeatability.
- Record counterevidence, not only successful examples.
- Do not invent comments or audience quotes.
- Distinguish evergreen interest from news-driven spikes.
- State clearly when data is insufficient.

### 10.3 Questions the research must answer

1. Which visual hook patterns recur among successful videos, and which appear overused?
2. How do strong videos differ from weak comparable uploads?
3. Do creators repeatedly succeed with distinct episodes, or depend on a few outliers?
4. Is there an identifiable gap for controlled, original, hypothesis-led simulations?
5. What level of visual fidelity is common in high-performing examples?
6. What can be produced with a small-team workflow without imitating competitors?
7. Which ideas can naturally support a meaningful long-form episode?

Do not claim that the product has a defensible competitive advantage until these questions have been investigated with documented evidence.

## 11. Pilot plan and decision gates

The immediate objective is to validate the editorial product and production feasibility, not to automate the entire channel.

### Gate 0 — Concept quality

Create a written concept for each proposed pilot. Reject any concept without:
- A distinct premise.
- A clear first-second visual.
- A defined rule or challenge.
- A visible payoff.
- A reason it is not just a recolor or copy.

**Pass condition:** Each concept scores at least 8/10 on the originality scorecard.

### Gate 1 — Technical spike

Build one minimal scene with placeholder geometry. Measure:
- Time to construct the scene.
- Simulation stability.
- Preview render time.
- Final render time at the chosen target quality.
- Memory/compute failures.
- Number of manual corrections.
- Export and audio-assembly time.

**Pass condition:** The scene is reproducible, renders without critical defects, and can be completed within a documented effort budget selected by the project owner. No runtime or speed is assumed in advance.

### Gate 2 — Three-format pilot batch

Create at least three distinct Shorts, one representing each pillar:
- Satisfying Systems.
- Physics in Action.
- The Experiment.

Use comparable quality standards. Record active labor separately from unattended render time. Do not over-optimize one video and compare it with two rough drafts.

**Pass condition:** All three pass technical, originality, rights, and human-review gates. This validates the workflow only—not market demand.

### Gate 3 — Initial publishing test

If the owner elects to publish, define the observation window and metrics before the first upload. Use comparable upload conditions where feasible, and document differences that cannot be controlled.

Record available YouTube Studio measures such as:
- Views and their observation date.
- Viewed versus swiped away, where available for Shorts.
- Average view duration and average percentage viewed, where available.
- Likes, comments, shares, and subscribers gained.
- Returning viewers and traffic information when the channel has sufficient data.
- Production time and cost per video.

Do not use a universal viral threshold. Compare videos with context, allow for small-sample uncertainty, and avoid drawing strong conclusions from three uploads.

**Pass condition:** There is enough consistent evidence to identify which premise patterns deserve another test. If results are inconclusive, produce a second controlled batch rather than declaring success or failure.

### Gate 4 — Repeatability test

Only after the first batch, produce a larger but still bounded batch of distinct episodes. Check:
- Whether new concepts remain available without superficial repetition.
- Whether production time falls through reusable components.
- Whether quality stays stable.
- Whether audience response repeats across more than one upload.
- Whether any pillar consistently costs too much relative to its observed response.

**Pass condition:** Repeatability and quality are demonstrated with documented measurements. No specific view count guarantees a pass.

### Gate 5 — Long-form decision

Plan a long-form episode only when one theme can sustain a coherent sequence of different experiments and meaningful explanation. Reuse the same visual assets and simulation infrastructure where appropriate, but add new value.

**Pass condition:** A long-form outline contains a central question, multiple distinct tests, a clear progression, and a conclusion. A compilation of Shorts alone is insufficient.

## 12. Metrics and decision framework

Track three separate dimensions. Do not collapse them into a single “viral score.”

### A. Audience response
- Initial viewing behavior and swipe-away measures when available.
- Average view duration and percentage viewed.
- Rewatches or looping signals when available.
- Shares, likes, comments, and subscribers gained.
- Repeat performance across multiple videos.

### B. Production economics
- Active human time per video.
- Total elapsed time, including rendering.
- Number of failed renders and manual repairs.
- Cost of software, compute, asset licensing, and audio.
- Percentage of scenes reusable without making episodes feel repetitive.
- Number of concepts rejected before production.

### C. Editorial quality
- Originality score.
- Physical/causal coherence.
- Readability on a phone.
- Sound and export defects.
- Rights and source completeness.
- Human reviewer’s pass/fail decision.

### Decision rules
- If audience response is promising but production time is too high, simplify the visual design or simulation method.
- If production is easy but episodes feel interchangeable, improve the premise and visual identity before scaling output.
- If technically impressive scenes do not hold attention, do not assume that more compute will solve the problem.
- If one pillar performs better in one video only, continue testing before changing the whole channel strategy.
- If the audience responds to the spectacle but not to explanatory labels, simplify the presentation; do not automatically add narration.
- If public metrics are too sparse or incomparable, record the result as inconclusive.

## 13. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Generic, interchangeable clips | Weak identity and monetization risk | Require distinct premises and originality scoring |
| Unconvincing or glitchy physics | Loss of trust and poor viewing experience | Preview renders, deterministic parameters, human inspection |
| Simulation takes too long | Unsustainable output cadence | Start with simple rigid-body scenes; benchmark before adding complexity |
| Over-engineering before audience validation | Wasted development effort | Gate all production automation behind pilot results |
| One viral outlier creates false confidence | Premature niche commitment | Evaluate multiple uploads and counterexamples |
| Public views mistaken for revenue | Faulty business model | Do not estimate actual RPM from public data |
| Copyright or license uncertainty | Takedown or monetization problems | Asset manifest and commercial-use review |
| Repetitive automated production | Monetization and audience risks | Human creative direction and episode-specific value |
| Long-form videos become padded compilations | Poor viewer satisfaction | Require a coherent central question and new value |
| Synthetic footage mistaken for real | Misleading content | Label simulations clearly when context requires |
| Inaccurate audience designation | Policy and feature consequences | Apply YouTube audience rules honestly to actual content |

## 14. Example concept cards

These are examples of the intended product shape, not approved scripts or production orders.

### Concept A — The Perfect Cascade
- **Pillar:** Satisfying Systems.
- **Hook:** A tightly packed arrangement begins moving from one small trigger.
- **Question:** Can the chain reaction finish in a precise final pattern?
- **Payoff:** The final pattern is completed—or a visible failure explains why it did not.
- **Distinctive element:** Original mechanism and intentionally designed final state.
- **Risks:** Generic domino imitation; too much setup; weak ending.

### Concept B — Same Drop, Different Surface
- **Pillar:** Physics in Action.
- **Hook:** Identical objects begin from the same height above three different surfaces.
- **Question:** How does the surface alter the outcome?
- **Payoff:** Side-by-side results with clearly stated settings.
- **Distinctive element:** Controlled comparison and consistent camera framing.
- **Risks:** Changing multiple variables; unrealistic materials; overclaiming real-world accuracy.

### Concept C — The Small Trigger Challenge
- **Pillar:** The Experiment.
- **Hook:** A small object faces a much larger system.
- **Question:** Can it trigger the full chain reaction?
- **Payoff:** The system succeeds or fails in a way the viewer can understand.
- **Distinctive element:** Designed causal path and escalating obstacles.
- **Risks:** An outcome that is obvious too early; hidden forces that undermine the premise.

### Concept D — One Rule Changes Everything
- **Pillar:** The Experiment + Physics in Action.
- **Hook:** Two visually identical setups appear side by side.
- **Question:** What changes when one parameter is altered?
- **Payoff:** A clear divergence in outcomes.
- **Distinctive element:** One controlled variable and a visually legible comparison.
- **Risks:** Unclear parameter, inaccurate labels, or differences caused by hidden variables.

### Concept E — A Loop That Solves Itself
- **Pillar:** Satisfying Systems.
- **Hook:** A mechanism starts in motion and appears to approach an impossible arrangement.
- **Question:** Can it return to its exact starting state?
- **Payoff:** A clean resolution or seamless loop.
- **Distinctive element:** Purpose-built timing and geometry.
- **Risks:** Looping that feels deceptive, repetitive, or has no meaningful resolution.

## 15. Final product acceptance criteria

A finished episode is not accepted merely because a render completed.

### Mandatory acceptance
- [ ] It belongs clearly to one of the three approved pillars.
- [ ] It supports the channel’s unified promise.
- [ ] Its premise and payoff can be stated in one sentence each.
- [ ] It passes the originality scorecard with at least 8/10.
- [ ] It has no score of 0 for original premise or episode-specific value.
- [ ] The main action is visible on a phone screen.
- [ ] The first second provides a meaningful visual reason to continue watching.
- [ ] The physical/causal sequence is coherent under the scene’s stated rules.
- [ ] There are no critical visual glitches or accidental export defects.
- [ ] Any factual claims are verified and appropriately qualified.
- [ ] Any artificial or stylized physics is not presented as measured real-world evidence.
- [ ] All external assets have documented rights and source information.
- [ ] Audio is clean and appropriately licensed or original.
- [ ] The video has been reviewed in full by a human.
- [ ] The episode adds something beyond superficial changes to prior uploads.
- [ ] Source project, parameters, and final export are archived.

### Rejection conditions
Reject or revise if:
- The premise is effectively a copied scene or a recolor.
- The outcome is not visible or the video cuts before the payoff.
- The simulation has major defects that affect interpretation.
- The scene is visually noisy or too small to read on a phone.
- The video makes unsupported scientific claims.
- The asset rights are unknown.
- The content is generated at scale with only superficial changes.
- The only argument for publishing is that it was cheap or fast to generate.
